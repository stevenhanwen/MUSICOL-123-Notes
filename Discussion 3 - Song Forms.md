# Simple Verse/Strophic
- Common in hymns, folk music, nursery rhymes
- Exact music and lyrical structure repeats throughout song
- AAAA
- Bob Dylan, "Blowin' in the Wind"
![250](assets/Screenshot%202026-09-21%20at%203.27.30%20PM.png)

# AABA
- Not compound AABA
- Old form, found in early opera and Tin Pan Alley
- Two repeats of same music and lyric structure
- One section of contrasting lyrics and/or music
- One repeat of initial music/lyrics
- AABA
- The Beatles, "Blackbird"
![452](assets/Screenshot%202026-09-21%20at%203.29.23%20PM.png)

# Simple Verse Chorus
- Characteristic of early Rock n Roll 1950s
- Alternate verses and choruses
	- often uses 12-bar blues
- Music is the same in verses and choruses
	- Melody may differ
- Chuck Berry, "Johnny B. Goode"
![483](assets/Screenshot%202026-09-21%20at%203.36.38%20PM.png)
![484](assets/Screenshot%202026-09-21%20at%203.37.33%20PM.png)

# Contrasting Verse Chorus
- Popular contemporary song form
- Alternating verses and choruses
- Music in chorus is contrasting
- Pre-chorus usually counts as a part of the chorus
- The Beach Boys, "California Girls"
![494](assets/Screenshot%202026-09-21%20at%203.38.42%20PM.png)

# Compound AABA
- Most common pop song form in use
- Combination of AABA and Verse Chorus
	- Example of last week: Paramore, "Brick by Boring Brick"
- Bridge must be high contrast and substantial
- Like simple AABA, but the A units have both the verse and chorus. 
	- within A, there is an A and B, but considered one 
- Typically you get at least that last chorus
- the default

# Other Common Forms and Edge Cases
- ABAC
	- Bing Crosby, "White Christmas" 1942
- AAB
	- Robert Johnson "Cross Road Blues" 1937
- Through-composed: no repeating sections. 
	- Queen "Bohemian Rhapsody" 1975
	- Common in "progressive" genres, e.g., progressive rock and progressive metal.
